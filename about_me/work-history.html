Case 1 · Salesforce

Salesforce Project Playbook

ResultThe Playbook became the reference model for large programs involving roughly 80% of Salesforce IT delivery teams.

Problem. Salesforce IT was growing rapidly across the United States, Ireland, and India. Large programs combined traditional program governance with Agile team delivery, but expectations, artifacts, and support handoffs varied widely.

What I did
  • At the request of the CIO and the Project Management Office (PMO) leader, authored a three-phase Playbook covering initiation, iterative delivery, operational transition, required artifacts, and support handoffs.
  • Balanced iterative team delivery with the enterprise controls leadership required, then turned the approved model into workshops, project-start walkthroughs, and adoption support.

The deeper value was reduced management complexity: leaders gained shared language and repeatable controls instead of treating every team and program as a separate exception.

Case 2 · Delta Dental

Delta Dental Planning and Product Investment Decisions

ResultCommon quarterly planning cycles across multiple teams fell from roughly three days to two, and technical work could compete for investment on the same terms as customer-facing features.

Problem. Quarterly planning was absorbing too much time because feature shaping, dependency discovery, and readiness work often happened too late. Technical work was often presented as a threat rather than as an economic decision.

What I did
  • Moved refinement, readiness, and dependency discovery into continuous preparation. One highly mature team reduced planning from approximately 3.5 days to one.
  • Reduced scope creep through clearer requirements, incremental validation, and earlier dependency identification.
  • Worked with Engineering Managers to frame business cases and quantify expected returns for technology-driven development and system upgrades, for stakeholder communication and tradeoff analysis alongside Product-driven business cases.
  • Used business cases and Cost of Delay to combine business and technical roadmaps into one ranked portfolio view.

Related: Write It Right, a whitepaper I co-authored with Tanya Jones and Karen Benbow on how one team fixed story writing upstream to shorten quarterly planning.

Case 5 · Delta Dental

Practical Scrum-to-Kanban Transition

ResultTested with several teams, then used to put six more teams on a path toward flow-based delivery.

Problem. Many transitions change board labels and meeting names without changing how teams manage waiting, handoffs, blockers, or work in progress (WIP). I developed a six-to-twelve-month model that treats Kanban as an organizational capability.

The approach
  • Begin with truthful active, ready, waiting, pending, blocked, and handoff columns.
  • Shift the daily stand-up toward flow, aging work, unresolved dependencies, and team ownership of blockers.
  • Introduce Expedite, Small, Medium, and Large swim lanes when the team is ready, rather than on a fixed calendar.
  • Use filtered control charts (how long each item actually took) to compare active and waiting time, isolate functional delays, compare like-sized work, and cluster recurring blocker causes.
  • Build cross-functional response capability before introducing meaningful WIP limits.

A detailed write-up of the full method is available on request.

How I Work

The method behind the cases, for readers who want it.

  1. Diagnose the system: Understand the actual workflow, decision paths, incentives, handoffs, and constraints before prescribing a framework.
  2. Frame the decision: Clarify what leaders or product teams need to decide, what evidence matters, and what tradeoffs are real.
  3. Design the mechanism: Create the roadmap, playbook, dashboard, workflow, business case, assessment, or operating model that makes the decision executable.
  4. Pilot and learn: Test the approach with willing teams, inspect the results, and adjust before broader adoption.
  5. Build capability: Teach the system, create reusable guidance, and transfer ownership so progress does not depend on the person who introduced the change.
  6. Reduce management complexity: Standardize the parts of the operating model that benefit from consistency while preserving local judgment where it adds value.
